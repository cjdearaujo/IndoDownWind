India · Winter air

Same emissions. Different air.

In two back-to-back winters, pollution moved from one half of India to the other. Emissions stayed broadly similar. The weather changed.

Scroll

The flip

Two winters, opposite outcomes

The two winters sat on opposite sides of a natural Pacific Ocean swing called ENSO.

2022-23 · La Niña

North India was remarkably clean, with PM2.5 down by up to 30% from the average of the previous three years. The peninsula was dirtier, and Mumbai spiked by 45%.

2023-24 · El Niño

The pattern reversed. Jaipur rose about 50% and Delhi by over 10%, while the peninsula got sharply cleaner.

Emissions and pollution-control policy did not change much over this period. The study finds an association with these weather patterns, not proof of cause.

The haze behind this page follows north India's winter air: thinner in 2022-23, thicker in 2023-24. The peninsula moved the opposite way.

The weather

Why the weather decides

Wind

Strong winds scatter pollution and carry it away. Weak winds let it pile up. That is stagnation.

Rain and pressure

Rain can wash pollution out of the air, but high pressure can trap it. In Jaipur, rain was tied to cleaner air in 2022-23, while in 2023-24 pressure mattered more and pollution spiked after rain.

A shallow lid

A shallow boundary layer squeezes pollution into a thin band near the ground, where people breathe it.

Where the air came from

Researchers traced the air back with back-trajectories. Jaipur kept receiving polluted northern air in the El Niño winter. Hyderabad received cleaner marine and southern air.

These are the mechanisms the study's authors describe. The study shows association, not proof of cause.

The map is shifting

The dry zone is moving east

Over 70 years, the hot semi-arid zone in northwest India grew from about a quarter of the region to a third, and pushed east into Uttar Pradesh, one of India's key farming regions. The timing of sowing and harvesting there needs watching.

In peninsular India the dry zone shrank from about 30% of the region to 16%, a change the authors attribute to rising rainfall over the south.

What this means

Plan for the weather, not just the smokestacks

Our takeaway: low local emissions do not guarantee clean air when the wind stalls, and strong winds can hide high emissions. So plans need to look beyond a single city and a single year.

What this study does not show

  • It shows an association, not proof of cause.
  • The two winters were unusual, so the pattern should not be assumed to repeat.
  • It does not say El Niño always worsens air in Delhi or north India.

Source: Times of India report on the study "Air quality in flux: The baffling ENSO transition in India" (Atmospheric Pollution Research), and an IMD/IITM study of India's climate zones.
